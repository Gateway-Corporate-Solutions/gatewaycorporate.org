I initially opposed the U.S.–Israel defense technology provisions in H.R. 8800. I misunderstood what they were intended to accomplish, and I let social media shape my opinion before I understood the legislation. That was my mistake.

After comparing the text with the preceding defense authorization law, I changed my mind. I now consider this combination of funding flexibility, joint development, and industrial cooperation vital to our national security.

Removing funding caps would be useless as a complete strategy on its own. So would tightening the relationship between our military research organizations without giving successful work a realistic path to production. Together, however, those changes establish a firm and reasonable roadmap for adapting to the technologies our adversaries are already using.

The distinction matters enough to explain precisely what changed.

The comparison here is between **S. 1071, the FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act enacted as Public Law 119-60 on December 18, 2025**, and **H.R. 8800, the House-passed FY2027 NDAA text dated July 22, 2026**. The latter is the proposal discussed below; its provisions should not be described as enacted merely because they passed the House. These are successive annual measures, and the newer proposal builds on the earlier law.[1][2]

The first thing I had to reconsider was whose problem this legislation addresses.

Countering drones is already an American military requirement. The Congressional Research Service documents the January 2024 drone attack in Jordan that killed three American servicemembers and injured more than 40, which Defense Department officials attributed to Iran-backed militia groups. Its assessment also describes the challenge of integrating counter-drone capabilities into American force protection and military operations.[3]

Underground warfare is similarly broader than any one conflict. The Army has documented North Korean infiltration tunnels beneath the demilitarized zone and the American and South Korean work required to discover them.[4] Tunnels are hardly a new invention. Our ability to detect and counter underground infrastructure still belongs in a serious discussion of modern military adaptation.

I see a clear American interest in developing these capabilities with a partner; especially one that is already working on these issues in-depth. The important question is whether that cooperation produces technology our forces can actually use, manufacture, maintain, and improve.

The earlier law already started connecting those pieces. **S. 1071 §1232(a)(2)(B)(i)–(ii)** broadened the counter-drone program toward accelerated development of technologies for countering unmanned systems across all warfighting domains. **§1232(a)(2)(D)** added joint research, training and information sharing, common technical requirements, industry participation, and coordination with acquisition offices. Those are the new **§1278(a)(2)(A)–(E)** of the FY2020 NDAA.[1]

That means the FY2027 proposal is not inventing cooperation from nothing. It would expand the resources and institutional support available to a direction Congress has already established.

Here are the most consequential changes for that effort. References in the first two columns identify the provisions in the two measures; references to FY2016 §1279 and FY2020 §1278 identify the older program laws they amend.[1][2]

| Program duration | **§1231(2)** and **§1232(a)(7)** extend the respective authorities through December 31, 2028. | **§1222(5)** and **§1223(2)** extend them through December 31, 2029. | Adds another year of authority. |
| Industrial integration | **§864(a)(1)–(2)** and **§864(c)(1)(A)–(B)** require a working group and assessment of deeper integration, including possible Israeli membership in the national technology and industrial base. | **§219(a)(1), (3), and (4)** directs an executive agent to facilitate technology integration, acquisition pathways, licensing, joint ventures, and U.S.-based coproduction or manufacturing partnerships. | Adds an implementation mechanism alongside the earlier study. It does not itself grant Israel membership in the national technology and industrial base. |

The funding tables give this more substance. Under **§4201**, program element **0603122D8Z**, S. 1071 lists $80 million for anti-tunneling cooperation and $70 million for the counter-unmanned-systems program. H.R. 8800 lists $100 million for each, alongside an increase in the emerging-technology-cooperation entry from $35 million to $50 million. These appear on **line 037 in S. 1071 and line 038 in H.R. 8800**; the tables are denominated in thousands of dollars and have no lettered subsection for these entries.[1][2]

Those are authorization figures, not proof that the money has been appropriated or spent. Likewise, deleting a program ceiling does not create an unlimited supply of money. It removes a particular statutory constraint on the support that may be provided.

For me, the most important provision is **H.R. 8800 §219**.

Its central value is the connection between research and use. **§219(a)(1)** calls for identifying jointly developed or Israeli-origin technology with potential utility in American systems and programs of record. **§219(a)(3)** addresses the transition into procurement. **§219(a)(4)** addresses the industrial arrangements needed to make that transition practical. **§219(a)(5)–(6)** adds coordination across Defense Department organizations and joint training and information sharing.[2]

That is the combination I missed when I first reacted to the bill.

A promising prototype needs more than another research grant. Someone needs to establish requirements, validate the result, resolve integration problems, and make it producible. Conversely, a well-organized research partnership cannot scale useful work if its financial authority is disconnected from the work Congress wants it to perform.

Either reform can produce isolated benefits. Neither, by itself, amounts to the adaptation strategy I want to see. Their value increases when they operate together: identify a shared military need, develop and test a response, establish a manufacturing relationship, and move a successful capability into service.

The proposal also acknowledges that these technologies overlap. **§219(b)(1)–(10)** covers unmanned systems, subterranean threats, missile and air defense, artificial intelligence and autonomy, directed energy and sensing, cyber defense and electronic warfare, biotechnology and medical defense, networks and logistics, industrial cooperation, and other jointly agreed emerging technologies.[2]

I read that as a reasonable framework for sustained adaptation. A counter-drone capability may depend as much on sensing, data integration, or electronic warfare as it does on the device that ultimately stops the drone. Organizing cooperation around those related capabilities makes sense to me. Whether the program succeeds will depend on execution, but the statutory direction is coherent.

Supporting that direction also requires being honest about its limits.

The subterranean amendment includes the ability to develop technology for **destroying** underground facilities. Calling it merely tunnel detection would conceal a material expansion. I support the broader capability, but my support rests on what **§1222(3)(B)** actually says.[2]

Nor does closer cooperation mean abandoning control of sensitive technology. **H.R. 8800 §219(a)(2)** requires protection of sensitive technology and information; **§219(c)** requires coordination to ensure consistency with existing laws and regulations. **§219(g)** requires public updates while protecting classified information, operational security, export controls, and sensitive technology.[2]

The previous law's emerging-technology authority contains more detailed conditions. **S. 1071 §1234(c)(1)–(2)** expressly subjects activities to export controls. **§1234(d)(1)–(2)(A)–(D)** requires an agreement and a prerequisite congressional report addressing cost sharing, American intellectual-property rights, expenditure reporting, and safeguards against access by China or other American adversaries. **§1234(f)** requires semiannual reporting. Its partner-country definition is in **§1234(g)(2)**; the authority is not exclusively an Israel provision.[1]

H.R. 8800's coordinating initiative does not reproduce that entire prerequisite structure, but it does not repeal §1234. Activities conducted under the earlier authority remain subject to its conditions. The omission of identical wording from a new initiative is not a general export-control waiver.

The two existing bilateral programs also retain substantive requirements. Their matching-contribution rules remain in **FY2016 §1279(b)(3)** and **FY2020 §1278(b)(3)**, subject to the specified exceptions. Their requirements to use at least half of American support for research, development, testing, and evaluation in the United States remain in existing **§1279(b)(5)** and **§1278(b)(5)**. H.R. 8800 **§1222(4)(B)** and **§1223(1)(B)** would renumber those domestic-spending provisions as paragraph (4), not delete them.[2][5]

Accountability is part of this arrangement too. S. 1071 **§1232(a)(6)** adds annual assessments of the counter-unmanned-systems program, including progress toward fielding. H.R. 8800 **§219(d)** requires an interim briefing within 180 days; **§219(e)(1)–(6)** requires annual reporting on activities, results, coordination, technology transitions, industrial partnerships, and future opportunities. **§219(f)–(g)** provides for unclassified reporting and public updates with appropriate protections.[1][2]

I would judge implementation against those obligations. Which capabilities reached American units? How much did they cost? Can American industry produce and sustain them? How quickly can they be updated when the threat changes? Those are the results that would justify the policy.

I changed my mind because these provisions fit together more coherently than the social-media discussion led me to understand. Funding flexibility supports development. Joint development supports shared requirements and testing. Industrial cooperation gives successful work a route into American production and procurement. Oversight gives Congress something concrete to evaluate.

I think that combination is vital to national security. Our response to drones, underground infrastructure, and other evolving military technologies needs to be organized around the ability to learn and adapt continuously. These provisions offer a firm, reasonable foundation for doing that with a partner while keeping American capability at the center of the effort.

I was wrong to form my initial position before understanding the relationship between those pieces. Having read them together, I support this direction and want to see it translated into capabilities that protect American forces.
